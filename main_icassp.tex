\begin{abstract}
Cross-corpus failure in audio deepfake detection is often a failure of the
decision threshold rather than of score ordering: a released detector in our
In-the-Wild evaluation has ROC-AUC 0.9171 but 39.08\% accuracy at threshold
0.5. Confident-tail self-training exploits the surviving ordering by
pseudo-labeling the lowest- and highest-scoring target clips, and we ask when
that rule helps or harms. A bucket assigned class $c$ and occupying fraction
$b_c$ of a pool with prior $\pi_c$ cannot be purer than $\min(1,\pi_c/b_c)$;
equivalently it must carry at least $N(b_c-\pi_c)^{+}$ wrong labels. Across 31
adaptation runs spanning four released checkpoints, two corpora and spoof
prevalence 0.01 to 0.97, that excess mass, and not purity, orders the outcomes:
below an empty gap 18 of 20 runs improve balanced accuracy by 5.7 to 32.7
points, while above it none of 11 improves by more than 4.3 and eight are
harmful. Accuracy at a fixed threshold exaggerates the effect. On
ASVspoof2021-DF, where always predicting spoof already scores 97.22\%, a
nominal $q=0.3$ budget moves accuracy from 98.87\% to 62.96\% but balanced
accuracy only from 88.28\% to 80.89\% and EER from 4.51\% to 5.84\%. A
black-box label-shift budget repairs the high-prevalence case, yet estimates
35.9--41.8\% spoof on pools that are 1.1--9.7\% spoof, where only a small
prior-free budget helps.
\end{abstract}
\begin{keywords}
audio deepfake detection, test-time adaptation, label shift, pseudo-labels
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Self-supervised speech representations paired with anti-spoofing back-ends
provide strong audio deepfake detectors~\cite{wav2vec2aug,aasist}, but transfer
across recording conditions and synthesis systems remains difficult~\cite{itw}.
A target-domain failure can involve both score ordering and the decision
threshold. For example, a released detector in our In-the-Wild evaluation has
ROC-AUC 0.9171 but only 39.08\% accuracy at threshold 0.5, which motivates
examining adaptation and re-thresholding separately without assuming that
ranking always transfers.

Test-time adaptation (TTA) uses unlabeled target observations to update a source
model. Entropy minimization~\cite{tent} and pseudo-label self-training can
reinforce incorrect predictions. We examine a specific self-training rule:
assign the bottom and top score quantiles to bona fide and spoof classes. This
symmetric-quantile rule is not one proposed elsewhere in the literature; it is
our own prior design, an instance of confidence-based pseudo-label
selection~\cite{fixmatch,shot} with a fixed budget on each tail. What follows
is therefore an analysis of a rule we had adopted ourselves. With a fixed
symmetric budget the minority bucket can exceed the entire minority population,
so no ranking can fill it correctly, which matters precisely when benchmark and
deployment pools differ in class proportions.

Class imbalance in adaptation is already established: Label Shift
Adapter~\cite{lsa} estimates target proportions to adapt model parameters, and
RLSbench~\cite{rlsbench} studies adaptation under joint label and conditional
shift; black-box shift estimation (BBSE)~\cite{bbse} estimates target priors
from a source confusion matrix, and T$^2$A~\cite{t2a} addresses deepfake TTA
through uncertainty-aware negative learning. Our contribution is narrower: a
counting constraint for confident-tail sampling, the quantity in which it binds,
and an audio checkpoint study of its consequences. We claim no new label-shift
estimator and no superiority to specialized label-shift adaptation.

\section{Tail selection and adaptation}
\label{sec:method}
Let $y=1$ denote spoof and $s_\theta(x)\in[0,1]$ the detector's spoof score.
The unlabeled adaptation pool has $N$ clips and unknown class fractions
$\pi_c$. Source labels are available for BBSE; target labels are used only for
evaluation, controlled resampling, and explicitly marked oracle analyses.

\subsection{A necessary condition for pure pseudo-labels}
A bucket $C_c$ assigned pseudo-label $c$, with realized fraction
$b_c=|C_c|/N>0$, contains at most $\pi_c N$ correct examples. Thus
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{purity}(C_c)\leq\min\{1,\pi_c/b_c\}.
 \label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
This is a finite-pool counting bound and makes no assumption about the model.
An update, however, does not consume a purity: it consumes wrongly labeled
examples. The same counting fact states that $C_c$ contains at least
\begin{equation}
 W_c=N\,(b_c-\pi_c)^{+}
 \label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
of them. Purity is a rate and $W_c$ a mass, and the two order configurations
differently: a small bucket can have low purity while contributing few wrong
labels. Section~\ref{sec:results} finds that $\sum_c W_c$ orders the observed
outcomes and that purity does not.

For exact symmetric tails, $b_0=b_1=q$, both statements reduce to
$\sum_c W_c=N(q-\min\{\pi,1-\pi\})^{+}$, so perfect purity is possible only if
\begin{equation}
 q\leq\min\{\pi,1-\pi\},\qquad \pi=\pi_1.
 \label{eq:necessary}
\end{equation}
At 97.22\% spoof prevalence a bucket labeling 30\% of the pool bona fide has
purity at most 9.27\%, and no amount of AUC removes that. Feasibility is not
sufficiency: score overlap contaminates feasible tails, and learning tolerates
impure labels when there are few of them. That is why we test
\eqref{eq:excess} rather than \eqref{eq:necessary}.

A prior allocation uses nominal fractions $q_c=2q\hat\pi_c$, keeping the total
selection fraction $2q$. With exact buckets and a correct prior, $q\leq1/2$
ensures $q_c\leq\pi_c$; with estimated priors it instead requires
$\hat\pi_c/\pi_c\leq1/(2q)$, a limit on overestimation rather than a safety
theorem. A smaller symmetric $q$ needs no point estimate, but a guaranteed
feasible nonzero value still needs a lower bound on the minority prevalence.

\textbf{Quantile ties.} Our cached experiments use inclusive score thresholds,
$s\leq Q(q_0)$ and $s\geq Q(1-q_1)$, with spoof assignments taking precedence
if masks overlap. Consequently, realized sizes can differ from nominal budgets.
Equations~\eqref{eq:bound} and \eqref{eq:excess} apply to the realized $b_c$;
the conditions above it describe exact-size tails. We audit
realized selections on cached source scores and do not interpret nominal
budgets as exact sample counts. Deterministic selection of fixed counts would
be a different implementation requiring new adaptation runs.

\subsection{Prior estimation and objective}
For $M_{ij}=P_{\mathcal S}(\hat y=j\mid y=i)$ and target prediction histogram
$h$, BBSE solves $M^\top\hat p=h$. We estimate $M$ using up to 4,000
class-balanced clips from the checkpoint's labeled training corpus; $h$ uses
source predictions on the adaptation pool. We clip the solution components to
$[10^{-4},1-10^{-4}]$ and renormalize. The implementation returns a uniform
prior if the matrix is singular. Because $M$ uses training rather than held-out
source data, optimism in its estimated error rates is a limitation.

At each of four passes, the current model scores the adaptation pool and
refreshes pseudo-labels. For a mini-batch $B$ and its selected subset $B_C$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal L_B ={}&
 \frac{1}{|B_C|}\sum_{x\in B_C}\!\mathrm{CE}(p_\theta(x),\tilde y_x)
 \\ &+\frac{\lambda}{2|B|}\sum_{x\in B}
 \|p_\theta(a(x))-\mathrm{sg}[p_\theta(x)]\|_2^2,
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $p_\theta=(1-s_\theta,s_\theta)$, the CE term is omitted when $B_C$ is
empty, and $\lambda=0.3$. The consistency term uses all batch examples and both
probability components. The perturbation is per-clip gain sampled uniformly
from $[0.7,1.3]$ plus Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.005. Only the
last four transformer blocks' LayerNorm affine parameters and the classifier
head are updated; BatchNorm statistics stay frozen. Adam uses learning rate
$10^{-4}$ and batch size 32. The released DeepFense models have 16,706 trainable
parameters under this rule. A fixed-confidence control instead selects scores
at most 0.05 or at least 0.95, with the same objective.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\textbf{Checkpoints and pools.} We use DeepFense XLS-R 300M + AASIST
checkpoints trained on ASVspoof2019-LA with training seeds 2, 42, and
240~\cite{deepfense,deepfenseweights}, denoted DF-2, DF-42, and DF-240. A
WaveFake-trained SSL-AASIST checkpoint, WF~\cite{shiftyspeech}, is evaluated
on In-the-Wild. Audio is 16\,kHz with the checkpoint-specific input length:
64,000 samples for DeepFense and 64,600 for WF.

The official ASVspoof2021-DF metadata~\cite{asvspoof21} has 533,928 eval trials
among 611,829 across three phases. Our available \emph{eval-only} subset is
400,435 of them, 389,299 spoof and 11,136 bona fide, or 97.219\% spoof, with
75.00\% class coverage of spoof and 74.89\% of bona fide. The missing shard
rules out a full-set comparison; all method comparisons use the same trials.

The main run adapts on 20,000 randomly selected clips with adaptation seed 0.
We report both the available pool, which includes adaptation clips, and its
380,435-clip disjoint remainder. Additional adaptation seeds 1 and 2 use a fixed
50,000-trial subset, selected with seed 12345, with 20,000 clips for adaptation
per run. These are sensitivity checks on overlapping data, not nine independent
datasets. In-the-Wild uses all 31,779 clips (11,816 spoof; 37.182\%), with
20,000 adaptation clips and an 11,779-clip disjoint remainder.

\textbf{Prevalence intervention.} On In-the-Wild with WF, we resample the pool
to target spoof proportions (sampling seed 4242): above the native 0.372 we
retain all spoof clips and thin bona fide clips, and below it we retain all
bona fide clips and thin spoof clips. Every resampled pool adapts on 8,000
clips, so the number of optimization steps is identical at every prevalence and
a disjoint remainder is always available for evaluation. The evaluation pool
size still varies with prevalence, and the experiment does not separate that
from prevalence itself.

\textbf{Metrics and controls.} We recompute metrics from saved float32
probabilities. ROC-AUC assigns half credit to ties; EER linearly interpolates
the crossing of FPR and FNR on the ROC, grouping identical scores. Interpolated
EER need not correspond to an attainable deterministic threshold. We also
report accuracy, balanced accuracy, and
\begin{equation}
 G_\tau=\max_t\operatorname{Acc}(s,t)-\operatorname{Acc}(s,\tau).
 \label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
The maximum ranges over all distinct thresholds, including constant
predictions. It uses evaluation labels and is a diagnostic of threshold
headroom, not a deployable method or a calibration error.

Two label-free controls keep source scores fixed and pick a threshold from
adaptation scores: their median, or their $(1-\hat\pi)$-quantile using BBSE.
Both preserve AUC and EER; neither is a claim of Bayes optimality.

We additionally report fixed-configuration entropy adaptation (Tent-style)
under the same update settings~\cite{tent}.\footnote{Three further published
TTA methods were run the same way, as controlled variants rather than tuned
reproductions: an ETA reliability-only ablation without redundancy or Fisher
terms (EER 12.31\%, and an all-spoof decision at threshold 0.5 on DF-42 and
DF-240), SAR with recovery threshold scaled to $(0.2/\ln1000)\ln2$ (4.48\%),
and IM-PL, a SHOT-inspired information-maximization control with
probability-space centroid pseudo-labels that freezes the head and updates
16,384 parameters (6.35\%)~\cite{eata,sar,shot}. None is a tuned reproduction
and none establishes anything about those methods' published performance;
their audited rows are in the released tables.}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Preventing large-budget damage on DF}
Table~\ref{tab:df} shows that symmetric $q=0.3$ lowers DF-2 accuracy from
98.87\% to 62.96\% and increases EER from 4.51\% to 5.84\%. Its oracle
threshold gap is 35.38 points: much of the fixed-threshold damage is
recoverable by re-thresholding, although AUC also declines. The BBSE budget
reaches 98.95\% accuracy and 4.06\% EER. Fixed confidence reaches higher
accuracy (99.02\%), and the smaller $q=0.02$ reaches 98.96\% accuracy and
4.19\% EER. Prior allocation is therefore one effective remedy, not a uniformly
best choice. Balanced accuracy is lower after BBSE adaptation than for the
source (82.34\% versus 88.28\%); the slight overall accuracy gain should not
be interpreted as an improvement for both classes.

The accuracy collapse and its reversal occur on all three checkpoints
(Table~\ref{tab:dfseeds}). Across the nine checkpoint/run combinations, BBSE
has lower EER than the symmetric budget in 9/9 on the available pool and 9/9
under disjoint evaluation, and lower than the source in 8/9 and 7/9
respectively. A paired stratified bootstrap over trials separates the two
comparisons: 2,000 replicates resampled within class, so each replicate holds
the pool's prior fixed. All nine symmetric deltas have 95\% intervals
excluding zero, but only 4/9 of the source deltas do on the available pool and
5/9 under disjoint evaluation. The DF-240 difference is $-0.02$ points
($[-0.14,+0.12]$) on the available pool and $+0.003$ ($[-0.12,+0.15]$) under
disjoint evaluation; neither is evidence of a gain. We give the counts as
descriptive, without treating reused checkpoints and trials as independent
evidence. Preventing severe damage is thus far better supported than improving
an already strong source.

The frozen-source BBSE threshold gives 98.88\% accuracy for DF-2, close to its
98.87\% source result, while the median gives 59.06\%. Median thresholding
implicitly targets roughly balanced predictions and is poorly suited to this
pool. The budget change can improve EER, which threshold changes cannot do,
but it is not required to preserve high accuracy here. Removing consistency
from the BBSE objective gives 3.98\% EER versus 4.06\% with it on DF-2; this
single ablation provides no evidence that consistency is needed for the gain.

\textbf{Score resolution.} Tent-style adaptation places 98.07\% of its scores
within $10^{-6}$ of 1.0 but only 93.02\% exactly at 1.0; conflating the two
would make its AUC appear impossible. With exact ties grouped it reaches EER
4.28\% and AUC 0.9765 against source 4.51\% and 0.9923, so an improvement near
one ROC operating point can coexist with worse integrated ranking. The scoring
convention itself matters: for the ETA ablation, ROC interpolation gives EER
12.31\% where a nearest-ROC-point rule would give 7.44\%. This supports
reporting several metrics, not dismissing EER as arbitrary tie-breaking.

\subsection{Corpus shift and prior estimation}
At the native In-the-Wild prior the symmetric budget has no counting
obstruction, and it improves accuracy on three of the four checkpoints; on DF-2
it does not, feasibility again being insufficient. For DF-42 it raises AUC from
0.9171 to 0.9473 and accuracy from 39.08\% to 82.39\%, though a frozen-source
median threshold already reaches 78.10\%: the comparison depends heavily on the
operating point. BBSE estimates 97.9\% spoof on this 37.2\%-spoof corpus and
lands at 14.54\% EER and 47.99\% accuracy, against 11.59\%/91.28\% for an
oracle budget that is not deployable and not always best, as on WF the oracle
gives 4.67\% EER against 4.55\% for the symmetric rule. BBSE is identifiable
under label shift with an invertible confusion matrix, even for uncalibrated
predictors~\cite{bbse}; under unrestricted conditional shift the prediction
rate $P_{\mathcal T}(\hat y=1)=\pi\,\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathcal T}+(1-\pi)
\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathcal T}$ alone cannot determine all three unknowns, which is
why a source confusion matrix may fail to transfer. That is not an impossibility
result for output-based estimators with further assumptions.

The large-budget degradation also occurs on resampled In-the-Wild
(Table~\ref{tab:skew}). At 97\% spoof, nominal $q=0.3$ permits at most 10\%
purity in an exact-size bona fide tail; its accuracy is 65.42\% against 98.59\%
for $q=0.02$ and 98.94\% for the unchanged source. Which budget looks better
depends on the metric, and the earlier claim that a small budget prevents the
collapse without improving the source holds only for accuracy at threshold 0.5.
In balanced accuracy the same cells read 81.8\% for $q=0.3$, 93.3\% for
$q=0.02$ and 83.5\% for the source, so the small budget is a substantial
improvement; in EER it is 5.21\%, 3.91\% and 3.74\%, so it is a slight
regression. Accuracy at a fixed threshold on a 97\%-spoof pool is the metric
that moves most and means least: the always-spoof rule alone scores 97\% there.

\textbf{The deployment direction.} Real pools are mostly bona fide, so we
extend the sweep to $\pi\in\{0.01,0.02,0.05,0.10\}$, where the constrained tail
is the spoof one. The mirror of the high-prevalence collapse appears: at
$\pi=0.01$ nominal $q=0.3$ moves accuracy from 65.72\% to 51.90\% and balanced
accuracy from 82.69\% to 75.71\%, and the damage is carried entirely by false
alarms (FPR 48.58\% against 34.62\%; FNR 0.00\% in both). The small budget,
however, does not break where \eqref{eq:necessary} says it must: at $\pi=0.01$
its spoof tail is bounded at 52.7\% purity and measured at 33.3\%, yet it
reaches 97.00\% accuracy and 92.36\% balanced accuracy. Ranking barely moves
anywhere in the sweep: every arm at every prevalence has EER between 4.95\% and
5.27\% while accuracy ranges from 51.90\% to 97.00\%.

\textbf{What orders the outcomes.} Fig.~\ref{fig:prev}(a) pairs each symmetric
cell's implied excess $\sum_c W_c$ with the balanced-accuracy change adaptation
produced. No cell lands between 715 and 1{,}427 excess clips. Below that gap 18
of 20 cells improve balanced accuracy by 5.7 to 32.7 points; above it none of 11
improves by more than 4.3 and eight are harmful. Worst-tail purity does not
order the same cells: 0.107 gives $+9.4$ points and 0.980 gives $-4.4$. The
barrier is one-sided. The two exceptions below the gap are exactly what
\eqref{eq:bound} cannot rule out, since feasible selection is not sufficient,
whereas no cell above the gap recovered. The separation is unchanged under
disjoint evaluation. It rests on one adaptation recipe and reused audio, and the
threshold's location inside the empty gap is not identified.

\textbf{Prior estimation in that direction.} BBSE, which repaired the
high-prevalence collapse, does not transfer here: it estimates 35.9\% to 41.8\%
spoof on pools that are 1.1\% to 9.7\% spoof, and changes accuracy by $-1.0$ to
$+4.7$ points against the source, where the prior-free $q=0.02$ budget gains
27.1 to 31.3. A small budget is the only rule in this study that helps at both
ends of the prevalence range.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
The counting obstruction gives a useful diagnostic for pseudo-label allocation,
while its consequences depend on score quality, realized selection sizes, and
the learning objective. Recomputed metrics support severe fixed-threshold
damage from large symmetric tails and its mitigation by prior allocation or
smaller tails. They also show important tradeoffs: some remedies reduce
minority-class performance, prior estimates can fail on shifted corpora, and
re-thresholding alone explains a substantial part of some accuracy gains.

The experiments use four released checkpoints from one broad architecture
family, two principal corpora, an incomplete DF eval partition, and fixed
adaptation hyperparameters. They do not establish general superiority over
specialized label-shift methods. Our prevalence intervention changes sample
count, and the seed checks share data. Future validation should use independent
corpora, fixed-size prevalence interventions, held-out source calibration,
exact-count tail selection, and development-domain tuning of baseline methods.
Neither a small nominal tail nor source-score agreement alone certifies safe
adaptation without assumptions about the target distribution.
