Adapting Public Detectors, Without Labels

Our test-time adaptation was applied to four independently released audio-deepfake detectors on the In-the-Wild benchmark. Every one improved. Two of our own prior claims did not survive the wider test, and those corrections are the most useful part of this report.

What this answers

Until now every number in the paper compared our source model against our baselines on our protocol. The predictable reviewer objection is “no comparison to state of the art.” Building a better detector is the expensive answer. The cheap one is to show our adaptation improves somebody else’s published detector, on a standard benchmark, using their released weights.

Because the method needs no training and no labels, this is plumbing plus adaptation runs — not a new model.

It is adaptation, not memorisation

The obvious objection to transductive test-time adaptation is that the model simply memorises the pool it adapted on. We tested this by adapting on one half of the corpus and evaluating on the disjoint half.

The adapted model performs the same on clips it never saw during adaptation. Whatever it learns is a property of the corpus, not of individual recordings.

Correction

The ablation inverts across models

Our working claim has been that the consistency term does most of the work. On the first checkpoint that looked right. Running the full ablation on a second checkpoint reversed it.

What this changes

“The consistency term carries ~89% of the gain” was a single-checkpoint inference and should not be published. The supported claim is stronger: neither component is sufficient, and the pairing is necessary. Self-training’s pseudo-labels anchor the objective; without that anchor the consistency term drifts to a degenerate solution. On ash56 the pair even beats the sum of its parts (−0.96 vs −0.78).

This is better evidence for the two-component design than either component being dominant. It also carries a methodological warning: any single-checkpoint ablation of this method should be assumed non-transferable until repeated on a second model — including the ablations already in the paper.

Correction

Tent’s collapse is real, but model-dependent

Our log records that naive entropy minimisation (Tent) collapses to ~49% EER. On the first public checkpoint it did not collapse at all, which briefly looked like a falsification. The second checkpoint settled it.

 AUC 0.502 is indistinguishable from random ranking.

So the ~49% figure reproduces on a third-party model — it is simply not universal. The invariant is not EER but AUC: Tent degraded the ranking on both models, mildly on one (where a recalibration gain masks it and EER appears to improve) and totally on the other. Our method improved AUC on both.

Recommended wording change in the paper

Replace the blanket “Tent collapses” with the mechanism: entropy minimisation degrades the representation; whether that surfaces as a modest EER gain or a total collapse depends on the model. That survives the counterexample a reader will otherwise find themselves.

Method

How we know the port is faithful

Both checkpoints build their XLS-R front-end through fairseq, which will not co-install against our pinned torch. We remapped the weights onto torchaudio instead, using torchaudio’s own fairseq key mapping — a pure state-dict function that needs no fairseq present. A silently broken port would make every adapted number meaningless, so it was gated before any GPU time:

  • Strict load. 429 fairseq keys → 421 tensors (the 8 dropped are pretraining-only), loaded with strict=True; back-end 245 keys with zero missing and zero unexpected, on all four checkpoints.
  • Not silently the stock model. All 421 front-end tensors differ from a fresh XLS-R init.
  • Score polarity. Public repos use [spoof=0, bonafide=1] — the opposite of ours. Measured: correct column gives 4.50% EER, flipped gives 95.50%. The flip raises no error, it just returns 100 − x.
  • Non-empty trainable set. Our parameter selector hardcodes our own module path and would have selected nothing on a foreign tree — a silent no-op that reads as “adaptation didn’t help.” Replaced with a generic search; asserts non-zero; finds 24 blocks, selects 18 tensors.

Independent corroboration: our measured source EER for ash56 is 5.04% on the full benchmark, against the 5.70% its authors report for an ensemble. A mis-ported model could not land there by accident.

A bug worth recording

A quoting error in our download script fetched the same checkpoint into two directories. It surfaced only because two supposedly different models reported byte-identical error rates — every affected run exited zero. An exit-code-only pipeline would have put a mislabelled checkpoint into the paper. The harness now verifies each checkpoint’s identity against its upstream config before loading.

Limitation

What cannot be claimed

  • Not a reproduction of a published number. Neither checkpoint has a published single-model In-the-Wild EER. Our 5.04% should be described as consistent with the authors’ reported ensemble result, never as reproducing it.
  • The ±0.02 is not a confidence interval. It measures adaptation stochasticity only — batch order and augmentation noise — because the checkpoint, corpus and evaluation set are all fixed. The genuine generalisation evidence is the four independent checkpoints, which is a much smaller n but the right kind of evidence.
  • The gain size is not predictable. It is positive on 4/4 but not monotone in source EER, accuracy or AUC — seed 2 sits between the others on every source metric yet gains least. This independently reproduces what our own earlier work found: no proxy reliably predicts the per-target gain.
  • One target corpus. Everything here is In-the-Wild. Whether this transfers to other target corpora is untested.
Decisions

What needs your call

Both items require editing existing claims rather than adding results, so neither was actioned:

  • The Tent framing in the paper — adopt the mechanism-based wording above, or keep the stronger claim scoped explicitly to our own source model.
  • The ablation section — whether to rebuild it around component complementarity, and whether to re-run the existing single-checkpoint ablations now that one of them has been shown to invert on a second model.
